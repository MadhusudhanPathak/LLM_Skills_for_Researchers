# Self-scrutiny

Use this move in two directions: to help the researcher examine their own reasoning for signs of motivated reasoning, and for Claude to audit its own contributions to the session before writing the audit.

## Part 1: the researcher's reasoning

### Use when

- The researcher is invested in a conclusion.
- Rival views seem to have been treated differently from the researcher's own.
- Near the end of a session, before revision.

### Stems

- What result would surprise you if your view were true?
- Are rival views held to the same standard of evidence as yours?
- Which assumption, if false, would you most want not to know about?
- What would make you abandon the view, rather than qualify it?
- Have you changed what counts as success since you started?
- What is the question underneath this question?
- If a colleague you respect disagreed, what would their best reason be?

### Patterns to look for

Describe patterns visible in the reasoning. Do not diagnose the researcher's motives.

- selective use of evidence or cases;
- asymmetric standards for supporting and opposing views;
- premature dismissal of alternatives;
- formulations that no possible result could count against;
- moving goalposts: changing the claim after each challenge without saying so;
- intuitions relied on when convenient and dismissed when not;
- loaded terms that build in the conclusion;
- conclusions stronger than the support.

For each pattern found: point to the specific passage or answer, offer a charitable explanation of how it could arise, say what risk it creates, and ask what a fair treatment of the opposing position would look like.

Also identify places where the work defends its thesis appropriately, so that scrutiny does not become indiscriminate scepticism.

## Part 2: Claude's self-audit

Run this before writing any audit, memo or report.

Check Claude's own contributions in the session for:

- factual claims made without support, or not verified;
- any reference, author, date or finding Claude mentioned that may be inaccurate;
- places where Claude misrepresented the researcher's material or answers;
- assumptions Claude introduced in reconstructions and did not mark;
- alternative interpretations Claude missed;
- conclusions Claude stated more confidently than the session supports;
- counterexamples Claude failed to consider;
- confusions between conceptual, empirical and normative claims;
- places where Claude followed the researcher's framing too readily;
- places where Claude criticised a claim without first reconstructing it fairly;
- places where Claude gave way to repetition rather than to a reason.

Correct what can be corrected in the audit. Mark what cannot be verified `[UNVERIFIED]`. Do not defend an earlier statement merely for consistency.
