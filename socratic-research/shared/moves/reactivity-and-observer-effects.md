# Reactivity and observer effects

Use this move when the thing being studied, tested or evaluated can notice that it is being studied and respond to that. People, groups, organisations, markets and adaptive systems of any kind (including software agents and machine learning systems) can behave differently under observation, under test, or once a measure becomes a target. A result obtained under those conditions may not describe behaviour elsewhere.

## Use when

- Participants, organisations or systems know, or could infer, that they are being observed or tested.
- A measure is used to reward, rank, select or train the thing it measures.
- Behaviour in a test setting is used to draw conclusions about behaviour in ordinary use.
- Performance is lower or higher than expected, and the reason is unclear.

## 1. Cues

- What distinguishes the study or test situation from the ordinary one? (Instructions, setting, format, presence of an observer, the kind of task, timing, metadata, consent procedures, unusual tools or permissions.)
- Could the subject detect those cues? Does it have the ability and the incentive to respond to them?
- Does the subject know what the researcher hopes to find?

## 2. Known forms

| Form | What happens |
|---|---|
| Observation effect | Behaviour changes because the subject knows it is observed |
| Demand characteristics | The subject infers what the study wants and supplies or resists it |
| Social desirability | Responses shift towards what looks acceptable |
| Experimenter expectancy | The researcher's expectations shape the subject's behaviour or the scoring |
| Test-taking strategy | The subject optimises for the test format rather than the underlying ability |
| Strategic underperformance | The subject performs below capability, for example to avoid a consequence of scoring well |
| Gaming of indicators | An organisation or system changes what is measured rather than what the measure was meant to track |
| Practice and familiarity | Repeated testing changes the subject, so later measurements measure something different |

## 3. Separating explanations

Test non-strategic explanations before attributing strategy.

- What would separate deliberate under- or over-performance from uncertainty, misunderstanding of the task, poor elicitation, fatigue, or ordinary variance?
- Is the target typical behaviour, best-effort behaviour, or maximum capability under assistance? Which matches the decision the result will inform?
- Was comparable effort spent eliciting the behaviour the researcher hoped to find and the behaviour they hoped not to find?

## 4. Reducing and detecting reactivity

- Can the study use unobtrusive measures, naturally occurring data, or field settings?
- Can cues be removed or disguised? Could removing them itself change behaviour?
- Can behaviour be compared across conditions that differ only in how obvious the observation is?
- Can consistency be checked across contexts the subject would not connect?
- Are there ethical limits on concealment, and how are they handled (consent, debriefing)?

## Watch for

- Test results treated as describing ordinary behaviour, without an argument that the test resembles ordinary conditions.
- A measure that has become a target still treated as a neutral indicator.
- Strategic explanations offered before simpler ones are ruled out.
- Interventions to remove cues that introduce new, unexamined differences.

## Artefact

**Reactivity threat model**: the cues present, the subject's ability and incentive to respond, the forms of reactivity plausible here, the tests that would detect each, and the claims that should remain conditional on reactivity being absent.
