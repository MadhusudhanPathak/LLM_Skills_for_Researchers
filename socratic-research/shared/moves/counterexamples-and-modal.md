# Counterexamples, thought experiments, modal reasoning and analogy

Use this move to test a claim against cases, and to examine reasoning that relies on imagined cases, on what is possible or likely, or on comparison with another domain. The aim is to discover the true scope of a claim, not merely to defeat it.

## Use when

- A claim, definition, principle, model or specification is precise enough to test.
- The work relies on a thought experiment, an intuition, or an imagined scenario.
- The conclusion moves between possible, likely, actual and guaranteed.
- The work transfers a conclusion from one domain to another by analogy.
- Stage 6 of the arc, and wherever a claim needs its boundaries found.

## 1. Precision first

A counterexample to a vague claim is a counterexample to nothing. Before generating cases, fix:

- the quantifier (all, most, typically, some);
- the modality (necessarily, possibly, probably, actually);
- the scope (which cases, settings, populations, systems or periods);
- the intended exceptions.

## 2. Counterexample families

Draw cases from several families. Each tests a different kind of weakness.

| Family | What it tests |
|---|---|
| Ordinary cases | Whether the claim gets the everyday instances right |
| Edge and borderline cases | Where the boundary of the claim lies |
| Minimal pairs | Two cases differing in one feature; whether that feature matters |
| Idealised cases | Whether the claim holds in the clean case it assumes |
| Extreme or limiting cases | Behaviour at scale, at zero, at the limit |
| Adversarial or strategic cases | Whether an agent trying to break or exploit the claim can |
| Temporal or dynamic cases | Whether the claim holds over time, or only at one moment |
| Behaviour-versus-mechanism cases | Same outward behaviour, different underlying process |
| Letter-versus-purpose cases | Satisfies the definition or specification but defeats its point |
| Right answer, wrong reason | The claim's verdict is correct, but not for the reason it gives |
| Cases the claim handles better than expected | Where it is stronger than its critics assume |

## 3. Assessing a case

For each candidate counterexample:

- Is it coherent and possible in the sense the claim needs?
- Is it relevant: does it fall within the intended scope?
- Is it **decisive** (the claim cannot accommodate it), a **pressure case** (it strains the claim), an **intuition conflict** (people disagree about it), or **out of scope**?
- If it succeeds, what is the smallest principled revision that blocks it without being ad hoc?
- What does the strongest response from the proponent look like?

Avoid contrived cases that depend on stipulations unrelated to the claim.

## 4. Thought experiments and intuitions

- Which facts in the scenario are stipulated, and which does the reader supply?
- What kind of possibility does the argument need: conceptual, metaphysical, physical, technical, practical?
- Does the case isolate the variable it is meant to test, or do irrelevant details drive the judgement?
- Change one feature at a time. Does the judgement change, and why?
- Does the wording of the case import the conclusion?
- Who is expected to share the intuition? Would a different framing, order or description produce the opposite one?
- What is the least evidential weight the argument needs this intuition to carry? What else could carry it if the intuition is weaker than assumed?
- Does the inference from the imagined case to real cases hold?

Do not dismiss intuitions wholesale. They can serve as evidence, as hypothesis generators, as constraints on theory, or as data to be explained. Distinguish a report that people have an intuition from evidence that the intuition tracks the truth.

## 5. Modal reasoning

- Where does the argument move from "could" to "will", or from "possible" to "likely"? What justifies each move?
- Does a possible failure demonstrate a meaningful risk, or only a remote possibility?
- Is one example being generalised to a class?
- Does the failure to observe something count against its possibility, its probability, or neither?
- What evidence could estimate the likelihood, or at least bound it?
- Are assumptions about capability, incentives, environment or time horizon made explicit?

An unknown probability is not a reason to dismiss a possibility, and possibility alone is not evidence of high risk or high likelihood.

## 6. Analogies

- Which structural features does the analogy rely on? Which differences does it hide?
- Are the shared properties functional, behavioural, structural, causal, or only metaphorical?
- Would the reasoning hold for a third case that shares the relevant features but differs elsewhere?
- What evidence would distinguish a real structural similarity from surface resemblance?
- Which inferences does the analogy support, and which does it not?

## Watch for

- Counterexamples to a claim nobody made. Check the claim's actual scope before pressing.
- Revising the claim to exclude each counterexample one by one, until it says nothing.
- Treating vividness of a scenario as evidence for it.
- Asymmetric use of intuitions: relied on when they help, dismissed when they do not.

## Artefacts

- **Case table**: each case, its family, what it isolates, its verdict (decisive, pressure, intuition conflict, out of scope), and the revision it prompted.
- **The three most informative cases**: the cases that most changed the claim, and the feature each isolates.
- **Calibrated modal statement**: the claim rewritten with the modality the support licenses.
- **Bounded analogy**: the analogy restated with the claims it supports and those that need independent evidence.
