# Steelmanning, objections and rival accounts

Use this move to build the strongest version of a position before testing it, to pursue objections through reply and counter-reply, and to compare rival accounts fairly. It applies to the researcher's own position and to every view they criticise.

## Use when

- Before any stage that applies pressure (the steelman belongs at the end of stage 2).
- The researcher is criticising someone else's view.
- An objection has been raised and its force is unclear.
- Two or more accounts, explanations, readings or designs compete.
- A debate seems to be going nowhere.

## 1. Steelman

- What problem does this position address, and what would its proponents say it gets right?
- What is its most plausible interpretation? Its strongest supporting reasons?
- Which apparent weaknesses are essential to it, and which are avoidable features of one formulation?
- Here is the strongest version I can state: [steelman]. Would an informed proponent recognise it?

Avoid attacking peripheral wording, implausibly extreme variants, or claims the position need not make. Distinguish what an author actually claims from common interpretations or later versions of the view. A steelman is not evidence that a view is true.

## 2. The objection ladder

Pursue one serious objection through three rounds rather than listing many.

1. **The strongest direct objection**, naming the premise, step or element it targets. Ask for a reply.
2. **The objection strengthened** in light of that reply. Should the reply be defended, narrowed, or replaced?
3. **The consequences of the revised position.** Does it create a new objection, an explanatory loss, or an inconsistency elsewhere?

## 3. Is the objection relevant?

Before pressing an objection, check what it actually hits:

- the claim itself;
- a premise supporting it;
- a stronger claim the researcher did not make;
- an implication that does not follow;
- a practical application rather than the claim;
- a possible but irrelevant edge case.

Then classify it:

- a logical refutation, an evidential challenge, a counterexample, a scope restriction, a practical concern, or a request for clarification;
- central or peripheral;
- answerable without changing the thesis, or not.

A count of objections is not evidence of weakness. Rank objections by seriousness and explain the ranking.

## 4. Comparing rival accounts

- Do these accounts genuinely compete, work at different levels, or combine?
- What common criteria can judge both without favouring either? (Explanatory scope, fit with evidence, simplicity, coherence, fruitfulness, treatment of hard cases, cost of revision.)
- Test both against the same cases. Do not change the test to favour one.
- Where do they make the same prediction or verdict for different reasons?
- Where do they diverge? Is there a case, observation or argument that discriminates between them?
- Is any proposed discriminator circular, assuming one account in order to test it?

## 5. Debates that talk past each other

- Is the disagreement about facts, values, definitions, explanatory aims, methods, or standards of proof?
- Do the parties use the same word in different senses, or different words for the same idea?
- What would each side accept as evidence? What would rationally move each?
- Which disagreements could clarification dissolve, which are empirical questions, and which are normative disagreements that evidence alone cannot settle?

Do not dissolve a real disagreement into semantics because the definitions differ, and do not assume the truth lies halfway between two views.

## 6. Refutation is not a positive theory

When a critique succeeds, ask:

- What exactly has been shown: an inconsistency, insufficient support, a counterexample, an explanatory failure, an unacceptable consequence?
- Which parts of the criticised view survive?
- Does the critique justify the proposed alternative, or only motivate considering it?
- What further argument or evidence would establish the alternative?
- Is there a third option that avoids the apparent dilemma?

## 7. The opposing view, taken seriously

When the researcher leans strongly one way:

- Why might an informed, reasonable person reject your view?
- What would they say your side misunderstands or underestimates?
- What evidence would rationally move you? What would move them?

## Watch for

- Straw men: the criticised view stated in a form its proponents would reject.
- Asymmetric standards: the researcher's view tested lightly, its rival heavily, or the reverse.
- Symmetry forced where one side has substantially better support.
- A steelman of the researcher's view that quietly changes it into something more defensible but different.

## Artefacts

- **Steelman record**: original position, steelmanned position, strongest objection, minimally revised surviving position, and the strongest unresolved objection.
- **Objection ladder**: the three rounds, with replies and the resulting revision.
- **Contrastive matrix**: rival accounts against common criteria and test cases, with the most discriminating unresolved question.
- **Dialectical map**: each side's claims, premises, strongest evidence, vulnerabilities, best objection to the other, best reply, and possible concessions.
