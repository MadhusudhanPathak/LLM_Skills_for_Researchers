# Argument reconstruction and inference

Use this move to make the structure of reasoning explicit and to test whether each step follows. It applies to any work that moves from reasons to a conclusion: a philosophical argument, the case for a theory, the justification for a design choice, or the inference from results to a claim.

## Use when

- An argument is written in prose and its structure is unclear.
- The conclusion seems to follow too easily, or not at all.
- A step changes strength, scope or kind between premises and conclusion.
- Several reconstructions of the same text are possible.

## 1. Structure

- What is the main conclusion? What are the intermediate conclusions on the way to it?
- Which premises are stated, and which must be supplied for the argument to work?
- Which premises are doing the most work? Which could be dropped without loss?
- Here is a reconstruction: [numbered premises and conclusion]. Which premise have I put in your mouth that you would not accept?
- Is there a different reconstruction that fits your text as well? Which one do you intend?

Mark any premise Claude supplies as a reconstruction. Prefer the reconstruction that best preserves the researcher's text, the force of the argument and the principle of charity.

## 2. Kind of inference

- Is this step meant to be deductive, inductive, abductive, analogical, statistical, causal, practical, or transcendental, or an inference to the best explanation?
- What principle licenses moving from these premises to this conclusion? Is the principle stated?
- Judge each step by the standard it claims. Do not fault a non-deductive argument for failing deductive validity unless deductive force is claimed.

## 3. Validity and following

- Assume the premises are true for the sake of argument. Does the conclusion follow?
- If not, what missing step would make it follow?
- Is there a countermodel or a case where all the premises hold and the conclusion fails?
- Does the argument assume what it sets out to prove? Is any premise the conclusion in other words?

## 4. Proportionality

The conclusion must not be stronger, broader or more certain than the premises allow.

- Does the step move from some to all, or from typical to universal?
- From could to will, or from possible to likely?
- From is to ought, or from describing to recommending?
- From correlation to causation, or from association to mechanism?
- From one case, model, sample or setting to a class?
- From a component to the whole, or from the short term to the long?

For each shift, ask what additional premise would license it, and whether the researcher accepts it.

## 5. Soundness

Separately from validity:

- Which premises are true, plausible, contested, or unsupported?
- Which would the intended opponent or audience reject?
- Is each premise supported independently, or only by the conclusion it is meant to support?

## 6. The weakest link

- Which single premise or step most threatens the conclusion?
- What would repair it? What would make it irreparable?
- What is the smallest change to the premises, definitions or evidence that would stop the conclusion following?

## Watch for

- Fallacy-labelling without analysis. Name the specific step and why it fails, not a Latin label.
- Reconstructing an argument into a weaker one than the researcher holds.
- Treating an enthymeme (an argument with an obvious unstated premise) as invalid when the missing premise is uncontroversial.
- Several conclusions run together; separate them before testing.

## Artefacts

- **Argument map**: numbered premises, intermediate conclusions and main conclusion, with stated and supplied premises marked.
- **Minimal argument**: the fewest premises needed for the conclusion.
- **Strongest charitable argument**: the best version, confirmed by the researcher.
- **Rival reconstruction**: any materially different reading, and how the evaluation changes under it.
- **Inference table**: each step, its kind, its licensing principle, and its status (follows, follows with a supplied premise, does not follow).
