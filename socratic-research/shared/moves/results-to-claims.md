# Results to claims

Use this move to check whether the conclusions written in a draft follow from the results, proofs, analyses or arguments that are meant to support them. Overclaiming usually happens at the writing stage, when the language of the conclusion drifts away from the strength of the evidence.

## Use when

- The researcher is drafting or revising conclusions, an abstract, a discussion, or a summary.
- A claim's wording seems stronger than its support.
- Stage 4 or 5 of the arc, for any category whose conclusions rest on results.

## 1. Claim by claim

For each claim in the draft:

- Which result, table, figure, proof, test, passage or argument supports it?
- Does the claim change any of the following relative to that support?
  - **modality**: could → does → must;
  - **quantifier**: some → most → all;
  - **causal status**: associated with → causes;
  - **population or system**: this sample → this population → all such cases;
  - **time**: observed once → stable → permanent;
  - **level**: component → whole; individual → aggregate;
  - **target**: the proxy improved → the thing that matters improved.
- If a shift occurs, what would license it? Is that stated?

## 2. The claim ladder

Place each claim on a ladder:

1. **Observation**: what was seen, measured, derived or read.
2. **Local inference**: what it shows about the cases studied.
3. **Mechanism**: why it happened.
4. **Generalisation**: what it shows beyond the cases studied.
5. **Implication**: what follows for theory, practice or policy.

Each rung needs its own support. Mark the highest rung the evidence reaches.

## 3. Classification

Classify each claim:

- **Supported** as worded.
- **Supportable after revision**: the evidence supports a narrower or weaker version.
- **Needs further evidence or argument**: the claim may be true, but nothing presented establishes it.
- **Unsupported**: the evidence does not bear on it, or counts against it.

## 4. Proportion and balance

- Do negative, null, mixed and contradictory results receive proportionate space, or are they relegated to a limitations paragraph?
- Are limitations stated where they apply, or only in a separate section that readers skip?
- Does the abstract claim more than the discussion? Does the title claim more than the abstract?

## 5. What the work does not license

- What conclusions might a reader draw from this work that it does not support?
- What is the strongest conclusion that is actually warranted?
- Which stronger conclusions remain unsupported, and what would they need?

## 6. Rewording

Ask the researcher to reword overclaimed sentences first. Offer candidate wording when they ask for it or after their attempt, labelled as Claude's suggestion.

## Watch for

- Hedged results paired with unhedged conclusions.
- Implications for practice presented as findings.
- "Demonstrates" or "proves" for results that suggest or are consistent with.
- A limitations section that lists generic limitations and misses the specific one that matters.

## Artefacts

- **Claim-evidence map**: each claim, its supporting evidence, the shifts between them, its rung on the ladder, and its classification.
- **Not licensed**: a list of conclusions the work does not support, including tempting ones.
- **Strongest warranted conclusion**: one or two sentences.
