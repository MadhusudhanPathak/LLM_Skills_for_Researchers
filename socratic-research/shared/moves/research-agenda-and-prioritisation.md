# Research agenda and prioritisation

Use this move when the researcher must choose between candidate projects or questions, sequence several pieces of work into a programme, or turn the open questions from a session into a plan. It is often the last move of a session, feeding the research tasks in the audit.

## Use when

- The researcher has several candidate questions or projects and must choose.
- A programme of related projects needs structure and sequencing.
- A session has produced many open questions that need ordering.
- Decision points for continuing, revising or stopping need to be set.

## 1. Programme or assortment

- Do the projects form a cumulative programme, or an assortment of topics?
- What connects them: a shared question, construct, method, theory or aim?
- Which result from one project changes what the next one does?
- What central uncertainty is the agenda designed to reduce?

## 2. Prioritising

Assess each candidate against explicit criteria. Do not pretend the ranking can be precisely quantified from weak evidence; give a transparent qualitative ranking.

- **Importance**: how much would the answer matter, and to whom?
- **Uncertainty**: how much is currently unknown? Is it already well studied?
- **Tractability**: can it be done with the methods, access and skills available?
- **Fit**: does it suit the researcher's skills, resources, supervision and goals?
- **Informativeness**: would every outcome teach something, or only success?
- **Follow-on value**: does it open further work?
- **Dependency**: does it unblock other projects, or depend on them?
- **Risk**: could it fail to produce anything usable? Could it cause harm?
- **Opportunity cost**: what else could the same time produce?

Then ask:

- Which assumptions drive the ranking? How does it change if they change?
- What cheap investigation would most improve the decision?

## 3. Turning open questions into an agenda

Group the open questions by kind: conceptual, logical, normative, empirical, formal, technical, methodological, practical. For each:

- what is unknown;
- why it matters to the central claim;
- what would resolve or reduce it;
- the kind of next step: reading, reconstructing an argument, formalising, building, coding, running a pilot, collecting data, consulting an expert, reframing;
- what result would change the direction of the work;
- whether it can be deferred without undermining the project.

Order by importance, diagnostic value, feasibility and dependency. Identify a first step small enough to begin immediately but informative enough to prevent wasted effort.

## 4. Decision points

For each project or phase:

- What milestone evidence shows it is on track?
- What would trigger continuing, revising, narrowing or stopping?
- How will null and negative results be used, rather than assumed away?

## Watch for

- Treating every open question as equally important.
- Rankings driven by novelty or interest rather than by what the answer would change.
- An agenda that assumes every project succeeds.
- A first step too large to begin this week.

## Artefacts

- **Priority table**: candidates against criteria, with the drivers of the ranking and the cheap investigation that would most improve it.
- **Dependency graph**: which projects or tasks depend on which.
- **Sequence**: the order of work, with milestones and decision points.
- **Stop conditions**: the observations that would end or redirect each line of work.
