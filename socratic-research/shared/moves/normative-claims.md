# Normative and evaluative claims

Use this move when the work says what ought to be done, valued, permitted, prevented or preferred, or when a value judgement is built into something presented as neutral: a metric, a threshold, a category, a design requirement, a recommendation.

Normative claims appear in every category of research, not only in ethics. A study that calls an outcome "improved", a system specified as "safe enough", and a taxonomy that sorts cases into "acceptable" and "harmful" all carry normative content.

## Use when

- The conclusion is a recommendation, a principle, an evaluation or a policy.
- The argument crosses from what is the case to what should be done.
- A threshold, trade-off or weighting decides the outcome.
- A definition, measure or category embeds a value judgement.

## 1. Kind of normativity

- Is the claim moral, prudential, epistemic, legal or institutional, aesthetic, or instrumental (what serves a given goal)?
- Is it about what is required, permitted, forbidden, good, better, or merely recommended?
- Is it meant to hold for everyone, for a role, for an institution, or for a particular situation?

Instrumental claims ("to achieve G, do X") are partly empirical. Separate the goal, which is normative, from the means-end link, which is a claim about the world.

## 2. From is to ought

- Where does the argument cross from a descriptive premise to a normative conclusion?
- What normative premise bridges the gap? Is it stated?
- Would someone who accepted all the descriptive premises but rejected the bridge be making a mistake? What mistake?
- Which empirical premises does the normative conclusion depend on? If one of them turned out false, would the conclusion change?

## 3. Foundations

- Which commitments about the nature of value or obligation does the argument need? Would it survive if those were denied?
- Does the argument rely on one ethical framework (consequences, duties, rights, virtues, contracts, care) in a way that its opponents would reject?
- Is there a version of the conclusion that several frameworks would accept? What does that version lose?

## 4. Whose values, aggregated how

- Whose interests, preferences or values count, and how much?
- How are conflicts between them resolved: by aggregation, by priority, by rights that cannot be traded, by procedure?
- Does the aggregation allow serious harm to some for small gains to many? Does the researcher accept that?
- Are interpersonal comparisons being made? On what basis?
- Whose values, at what time? If values are fixed now, how does the argument treat later moral change?

## 5. Applying the principle

- Where does applying the principle produce paralysis, with no permissible option?
- Where does it produce a result its proponents would reject?
- Is the principle action-guiding: can someone in the situation tell what it requires?
- How does it handle uncertainty about the facts, and uncertainty about which moral view is right?

## 6. Values presented as description

- Does any definition, category, metric or threshold carry a value judgement that is presented as neutral?
- Who chose the threshold, and who bears the cost of each kind of error it permits?
- Would a different stakeholder draw the line elsewhere? On what grounds?
- Is a contested normative choice hidden inside a technical or methodological decision?

## 7. Distribution and consequences

- Who gains and who loses if the recommendation is adopted?
- Are the costs borne by those who choose, or by others?
- Are there people affected who have no voice in the decision?
- What are the second-order effects: incentives created, practices displaced, precedents set?

## Watch for

- An experiment or data analysis presented as settling a normative question. Evidence can inform a normative conclusion only through a stated bridge premise.
- A normative claim defended only by intuition, without testing whether the intuition survives redescription.
- Moral language used for emphasis where no normative claim is intended.
- A recommendation that rests on an unstated empirical forecast.

## Artefacts

- **Normative bridge map**: each normative conclusion, the descriptive premises it relies on, the bridge principle connecting them, and the status of each.
- **Value-ladenness register**: each definition, measure, threshold or category that embeds a value judgement, the judgement it embeds, and who would contest it.
- **Stakeholder table**: who is affected, how, whether they have a voice, and how the argument weighs them.
