# Research question, contribution and feasibility

Use this move to examine the question the work is trying to answer, what it would contribute, whether it can change anything downstream, and whether it can be done with the time and resources available.

This is **not a default stage of the arc**. The arc examines the work as it stands. Draw on this move only when the intake shows the question is unsettled, when the researcher asks whether they are asking the right question, when stages keep failing in a way that traces back to the question, or when the session must judge whether a plan is achievable.

## Use when

- The research question is new, broad, shifting, or contested.
- The contribution is asserted rather than stated.
- The work is meant to change a decision, practice or policy, and the path to that change is unclear.
- A deadline, budget or access constraint binds.
- The researcher must choose between candidate projects (see also `research-agenda-and-prioritisation.md`).

## 1. The question

- What problem is this question meant to solve, and for whom?
- Does it presuppose an answer, or build in a false dichotomy ("is X a Y or a Z?", where Y and Z may be neither exclusive nor exhaustive)?
- Is it one question, or several that should be separated?
- Is it conceptual, normative, explanatory, empirical, technical, or a mix? Which parts can the intended methods answer?
- What would count as an answer? What would a satisfying answer look like on one page?
- Is the unit of analysis right? Is the level of abstraction right?
- Is it answerable with the sources, data, methods and access available?
- Would it still be worth asking if the expected answer turned out false?
- Is there a more fundamental question that must be answered first? A more decision-relevant one that avoids unnecessary abstraction?

## 2. Contribution

- In one sentence, what will a reader know after this work that they do not know now?
- If the sentence needs "explore", "investigate" or "shed light on", the contribution is not yet defined. What claim would the result support?
- What kind of contribution is it: a new question, distinction, argument, method, synthesis, application, dataset, artefact, result, or a test of an untested assumption?
- What would a negative or null result show? A design where every outcome is informative is safer on a fixed timeline.
- Who judges the contribution, and what do they reward? Examiners reward a clear question answered carefully; venue reviewers reward novelty and significance; funders reward feasibility and fit with their call; practitioners reward usefulness.

## 3. Theory of change

When the work is meant to change something beyond the page:

Trace the chain: activity, output, uptake, changed practice or decision, outcome. For each arrow:

- What is the mechanism by which one leads to the next?
- What evidence supports that link?
- Which actors must act, and what are their incentives?
- What is the most likely way this link fails?
- Would a technically sound result actually change the decision it targets?

Separate activity measures (papers written, tools built), quality measures (validity, rigour), uptake measures (adoption, citation in practice), and evidence of real effect.

Ask also: could another project answer the same decision question more cheaply or directly? Could the work create false confidence, or cause harm if misused?

## 4. Feasibility

The binding constraint is usually time, then access (data, texts, compute, participants, equipment), then skills.

- **Count backwards from the deadline.** Writing, feedback and revision come last and take longer than planned. What remains is the budget for the work itself.
- **Find the critical path.** Ethics approval, data-access agreements, archive visits, equipment bookings and compute allocations can take weeks and cannot be hurried.
- **Find the hardest step.** Is it known-hard (others have done it; it takes effort) or novel-hard (nobody knows if it can be done)? A plan that rests on a novel-hard step needs a fallback that still gives a result.
- **Count the dependencies.** Several things that must all work at once multiply the chance of failure.
- **Find the minimum version.** What is the smallest version of the work that tests the riskiest assumption and still produces something reportable?

## 5. Three formulations

After the researcher has proposed their own revision, ask them to compare three formulations:

- **Narrow and executable**: certain to finish, modest contribution;
- **Moderate**: a real contribution with manageable risk;
- **Ambitious**: the most interesting version, with a stated fallback.

For each: the evidence or argument it needs, what it excludes, the likely problems, what a null result would mean, and the contribution it could support.

## Watch for

- A difficult question mistaken for a bad one.
- A question narrowed until nobody would care about the answer.
- Scoping to the most interesting version when a deadline binds.
- A contribution claim pitched at the strength the researcher hopes for, not the strength the evidence could support.

## Artefacts

- **Question statement**: the question in one sentence, every term defined, with its kind and unit of analysis.
- **Contribution claim**: one sentence, plus a more cautious fallback, plus what a null result would still show.
- **Impact chain**: each arrow with its mechanism, evidence, actors and weakest point.
- **Feasibility check**: budget after writing time, critical path, hardest step and its type, dependencies, minimum version, fallback.
