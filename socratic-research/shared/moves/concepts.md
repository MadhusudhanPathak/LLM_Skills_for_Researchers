# Concepts, definitions and classification

Use this move when a term carries weight in the work: when the conclusion depends on what a word means, when a definition is proposed or contested, when cases must be sorted into kinds, or when a concept is being revised for a purpose.

Stems are grouped by angle so that a round on one concept can approach it from four or five directions. Adapt every stem to the researcher's own term and material.

## Use when

- A load-bearing term is vague, contested, technical, or used in more than one sense.
- A definition, criterion or set of conditions has been proposed.
- The work classifies cases into types, or builds a framework or taxonomy.
- The researcher proposes to sharpen, revise, split or replace a concept.
- A disagreement may turn on different meanings of the same word.

## 1. The work the concept does

- What does this concept do in your argument? What would break if you removed it?
- What problem was this concept introduced to solve, and for whom?
- Is it doing explanatory work, classificatory work, evaluative work, or several at once?
- Does it identify a mechanism, or does it label a pattern? What would it predict or rule out that simpler vocabulary would not?
- What would be lost if you replaced the term with two more precise ones?

## 2. The kind of concept

- Is the concept descriptive, normative, functional, dispositional, behavioural, structural, relational, or a mixture?
- Is it meant to pick out a natural category, a stipulated research construct, or a target for revision?
- Does it come in degrees, or is it all-or-nothing? If degrees, where are the thresholds, and why there?
- Is it defined by what something is, what it does, what it is for, or how it came about?
- Is it a cluster concept, where no single feature is necessary?

## 3. Cases

- Give a clear case, a clear non-case, and a case you are unsure about.
- What is the most central example, and what makes it central?
- Here is a borderline case: [case]. Does it fall under the concept? What in your definition decides it?
- Is there a case that satisfies the letter of your definition but clearly misses its point?
- Is there a case that everyone would call an instance, which your definition excludes?

## 4. Neighbours and contrasts

- Which neighbouring concept must this one be distinguished from? What is the difference?
- Could the same phenomenon be described with a different concept? What would change?
- Do other fields use this term differently? Does your argument depend on one field's sense?

## 5. Testing a definition

After each candidate definition, test:

- **Extension**: does it include exactly the cases it should?
- **Intension**: does it capture what makes them cases?
- **Circularity**: does it use the term being defined, or a synonym, in the definition?
- **Vacuity**: does it include everything, so that it distinguishes nothing?
- **Over-inclusion**: what does it let in that it should not?
- **Under-inclusion**: what does it leave out that it should include?
- **Operability**: can someone else apply it to a new case and reach the same verdict?

Stems:

- Can the concept apply while this condition is absent? (necessity)
- Can this condition hold while the concept does not apply? (sufficiency)
- Is this condition constitutive of the concept, or only evidence for it, or a typical cause of it?
- Would two careful people applying this definition to the same case agree?

Test conditions one at a time. Rejecting a whole account because one condition fails throws away the conditions that survived. Before accepting a proposed counterexample, ask whether it is possible, relevant, and within the intended scope.

## 6. Ambiguity and equivocation

- In premise [N] and the conclusion, does this word mean the same thing?
- Paraphrase each use without the word. Does the inference still go through?
- Is the shift doing work in the argument, or is it harmless?

Common shifts to check:

- behavioural versus internal;
- actual versus dispositional or potential;
- descriptive versus normative;
- part versus whole; individual versus aggregate; component versus system;
- type versus token; process versus product;
- ordinary versus technical sense;
- the sense in the source literature versus the sense in the researcher's work.

A word with several meanings is a problem only when the shift does illicit work in an inference. Establish that before calling it an equivocation.

## 7. Category mistakes and reification

- Is a metaphor being treated as a literal mechanism?
- Is a property of the measure being attributed to the thing measured?
- Is a property of a component being attributed to the whole system, or the reverse?
- Is a statistical or aggregate pattern being treated as an entity with its own causal powers?
- Is a mental, social or intentional term being applied to something without stating the functional or evidential basis for applying it?

Distinguish a genuine category mistake from legitimate abstraction, analogy or shorthand.

## 8. Classification and taxonomy

Use when the work divides a domain into kinds.

- What is the principle of division? Is one principle used throughout, or does it change between levels?
- Is the scheme meant to be exhaustive? Is everything in the domain placed somewhere?
- Is it meant to be mutually exclusive? Can anything fall into two classes?
- Is the grain right: too coarse to discriminate cases that matter, or too fine to be usable?
- What is the classification for? Would a different purpose give a different scheme, and is that acceptable?
- Are the categories defined by necessary and sufficient conditions, by prototypes, or by family resemblance?
- How are hybrid, borderline and novel cases handled?
- Is there an "other" category, and is it doing too much work?

## 9. Conceptual engineering

Use when the researcher proposes to improve, precisify, revise or replace a concept.

- Is the proposal ameliorative (better serves a purpose), precisifying (sharper boundaries), revisionary (changes what falls under it), or eliminative (drops it)?
- What defect in the existing concept has been shown, not just asserted?
- Does the revision change the subject rather than improve the concept? Would users of the old term recognise the new one as the same concept?
- Whose purposes decide whether the revision succeeds? Who gains and who loses under the new classification?
- Compare at least three options: retain the concept, revise it, or split it into several narrower concepts. What does each cost?
- What are the success criteria: clarity, discrimination between cases, fruitfulness, tractability, communicability, resistance to misuse?

## Watch for

- Ordinary usage treated as settling a technical or normative question. Usage is evidence about a concept, not a verdict.
- A definition that makes the conclusion true by stipulation.
- A definition that is precise but useless for the stated purpose.
- A demand for a perfectly sharp definition where the concept is legitimately vague. Not every useful concept has necessary and sufficient conditions.
- A dispute that looks substantive but is about words, or that looks verbal but hides a real disagreement.

## Artefacts

- **Definition ledger**: one row per candidate definition, with its purpose, strongest counterexample, revision made, and current status. Ends with the narrowest surviving definition and its remaining indeterminacies.
- **Condition matrix**: each condition against test cases, marking necessary, sufficient, typical or evidential.
- **Glossary of stable meanings**: each load-bearing term with the single sense used, and the inferences that had to be revised to keep it stable.
- **Taxonomy check**: principle of division, coverage, exclusivity, grain, hard cases, and their treatment.
