# Hypotheses, causal claims and models

Use this move when the work proposes an explanation: a hypothesis, a causal claim, a mechanism, a theory or a formal model of some phenomenon. It covers turning a problem into testable form, examining causal reasoning, judging a model's assumptions and virtues, and setting out in advance what would count against the account.

## Use when

- A problem needs to become testable.
- The work claims that one thing causes, explains or produces another.
- A theory or model is proposed, extended or compared with rivals.
- The researcher needs to state what would change their mind.

## 1. From problem to hypothesis

For each candidate hypothesis, require:

- the unit of analysis;
- what varies (independent variables, conditions or interventions) and what is affected (outcomes);
- the proposed mechanism;
- the expected direction or pattern;
- the scope conditions under which it is meant to hold;
- observations that would reduce confidence in it;
- rival hypotheses that predict the same surface result;
- a test that would discriminate between them;
- what a null result would mean.

Stems:

- What observable difference would there be if your account were right rather than the closest rival?
- Which parts of this are conceptual or normative and cannot be tested empirically at all?
- Which premises are conceptual truths, which are empirical assumptions, and which are modelling choices?

Do not force a conceptual or normative claim into an empirical hypothesis. It belongs to the conceptual or normative moves instead.

## 2. Causal claims

- Is the claim correlational, predictive, causal, explanatory, or about what an intervention would do?
- What is the implied causal structure? Draw it: causes, outcomes, and the paths between them.
- What could cause both the supposed cause and the outcome (confounders)?
- What lies on the path between them (mediators)? What changes the strength of the effect (moderators)?
- Could conditioning on something have created a spurious association (collider or selection effects)?
- Could the causation run the other way?
- Does the evidence identify the mechanism, or only fit it?
- What intervention or comparison would distinguish the causal account from its rivals?

## 3. Mechanisms

- What are the parts, their activities, and how are they organised to produce the outcome?
- At what level is the mechanism described? Would a lower or higher level explain more?
- Is the mechanism inferred from the outcome it explains? What independent evidence is there for it?
- What else does the mechanism predict, beyond the phenomenon it was introduced to explain?

## 4. Theories and models

- What phenomena is the theory meant to explain? Which does it leave out, and does it claim to?
- What are its core commitments, and what are its auxiliary assumptions?
- Which assumptions are idealisations (known to be false, adopted for tractability)? Do the conclusions depend on them?
- Are the results robust: do they survive changes to the assumptions that are not essential to the theory?
- Is the theory consistent with established results in neighbouring areas? If not, which gives way?
- Does it unify phenomena previously explained separately?
- Is it parsimonious, or does it add entities, parameters or mechanisms that do no work?
- Is it fruitful: does it suggest new questions, tests or applications?

## 5. Falsification and update conditions

- What would count against the account: an observation, a result, an argument, a superior rival?
- Can the account accommodate every possible result? If so, has it become empty?
- What evidence would increase confidence? Decrease it? Require a narrower scope? Motivate abandoning the view? Be irrelevant?
- Can auxiliary assumptions always absorb a failure? How would you tell a principled revision from an ad hoc rescue?
- What result would surprise you if the account were true?

Ask the researcher to commit, before seeing further evidence, to how each possible result would change the claim.

## Watch for

- A hypothesis so flexible that any outcome confirms it.
- A mechanism that is only a redescription of the outcome ("it happens because of a tendency to happen").
- A model whose conclusions are driven by an idealisation the researcher has not examined.
- Causal language ("drives", "leads to", "because") attached to correlational evidence.
- Confirmation sought only in the cases where the hypothesis was developed.

## Artefacts

- **Hypothesis tree**: primary, auxiliary, rival and null hypotheses, each with its prediction, and the smallest test that separates the most consequential pair.
- **Causal diagram**: variables and paths, with confounders, mediators, moderators and colliders marked, and the assumptions it encodes.
- **Model assumption ledger**: each assumption, whether it is core, auxiliary or an idealisation, and whether the conclusions are robust to relaxing it.
- **Pre-commitment statement**: possible findings linked in advance to how each would change the claim.
