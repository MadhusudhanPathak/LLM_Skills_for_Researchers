# Claim sorting

Before questioning starts, sort the material into claims and identify what kind each claim is. Each kind is answerable to a different standard of support. Holding a conceptual thesis to experimental standards, or treating an experiment as if it settled a normative question, wastes the session and misplaces the criticism.

Claim sorting is used three times in a session: at intake (to check the category fit), during clarification (stage 1 of the arc), and in the audit (the full claim inventory).

## Kinds of claim

| Kind | What it asserts | Typical form | What supports it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conceptual | What something is, what a term means, how concepts relate | "X is Y", "X consists in...", "X differs from Z in..." | Analysis, cases and counterexamples, coherence with usage and purpose |
| Classificatory | How things divide into kinds | "There are three types of...", "A belongs with B" | Principle of division, coverage, exclusivity, usefulness |
| Normative | What ought to be done, valued or permitted | "We should...", "X is wrong / good / fair" | Argument from principles and considered judgements, plus any empirical premises it relies on |
| Metaphysical | What exists, what is possible or necessary | "X is possible", "X is nothing over and above Y" | Argument, modal reasoning, theoretical virtues |
| Epistemic | What can be known, by whom, with what justification | "We cannot know X from Y" | Argument about evidence and access, sometimes empirical premises |
| Interpretive | What a text, source, record or artefact means | "Author A holds...", "This passage implies..." | Textual evidence, context, competing readings |
| Formal | What follows within a formal system | "Theorem: ...", "Under assumptions A, B follows" | Proof, counterexample, model checking |
| Theoretical / explanatory | Why something happens, by what mechanism | "X occurs because...", "Model M explains..." | Explanatory power, coherence, derived predictions and their tests |
| Empirical / descriptive | What is observed to be the case | "X occurs in N% of...", "Group A scored higher" | Observation, measurement, data, with validity and inference checks |
| Causal | That one thing brings about another | "X causes / increases / prevents Y" | Designs that rule out rival explanations, interventions, mechanism evidence |
| Predictive | What will happen | "X will...", "Under conditions C, expect..." | Track record, model validity, stated assumptions |
| Technical / design | That an artefact, method or system does something or meets a requirement | "The algorithm guarantees...", "The system handles..." | Specification, proof, testing, benchmarking against fair baselines |
| Practical / recommendation | What someone should do in a given situation | "Practitioners should...", "We recommend..." | A normative premise plus empirical premises about consequences and feasibility |

A single sentence can contain several kinds. "Since engagement metrics reward outrage, platforms ought to drop them" contains an empirical causal claim and a normative conclusion, linked by an unstated bridge premise.

## Strength and scope

For every load-bearing claim, record:

- **Quantifier**: all, most, typically, some, at least one.
- **Modality**: necessarily, possibly, probably, actually.
- **Scope**: which cases, populations, systems, periods or conditions it covers.
- **Status**: stated by the researcher, implied by their material, or supplied by Claude's reconstruction.

Two claims that look alike can differ completely in what they require. "Some definitions of X are circular" needs one example; "every definition of X is circular" needs an argument.

## Procedure

1. List the claims in the material, using the researcher's own words where possible.
2. Tag each with its kind, quantifier, modality and scope.
3. Mark the **load-bearing** claims: those the conclusion depends on, such that if they fell the conclusion would fall or change.
4. Mark the **bridges**: claims that connect one kind to another (an empirical premise supporting a normative conclusion; a definition used to classify an observation; a formal result applied to a real system). Bridges are where most misfits hide.
5. Note which claims belong to the invoked category and which belong to another. This feeds the category fit check at intake.

## Misfits to watch for

- An experiment presented as settling a conceptual or normative question. It can inform such a question only through a stated bridge premise, and the bridge is then open to examination.
- A definition presented as a discovery, so that a conclusion follows by stipulation.
- A formal result presented as a real-world guarantee without an argument that the formal assumptions hold.
- A correlation presented as a mechanism, or a mechanism presented as established by its plausibility.
- A normative conclusion presented as neutral description (a value judgement built into a metric, threshold or category).
- A prediction presented as an explanation, or the reverse.
- An interpretation of a source presented as what the source says.

When you find one, name the two kinds involved and the bridge that is missing. Do not dismiss the claim; ask what would connect them.

## Output

At intake, a short list of the main claims with kind and load-bearing marks. In the audit, the full inventory as a table:
