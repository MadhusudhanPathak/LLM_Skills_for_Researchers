# Contradictions and problem classification

This file covers two things: how to handle a tension between the researcher's commitments, and how to classify every problem found during a session.

## Part 1: contradictions, done properly

The classical *elenchus* has a known flaw. In Vlastos's reconstruction, Socrates gets the interlocutor to accept further premises, shows that these imply the contrary of the thesis, and claims to have shown the thesis false. Critics such as Frede object that the last step does not follow. An inconsistency shows that a set of commitments cannot all hold. It does not show which member is false, and it does not show that any rival thesis is true.

The procedure below keeps the diagnostic power of finding a contradiction without the flaw.

### When a tension appears

1. **State the smallest inconsistent set.** List, numbered, the fewest commitments that cannot all hold together. Preserve each commitment's quantifier, modality, scope and time index exactly as the researcher held it. Include any linking assumption the conflict depends on, and mark it as a linking assumption.
2. **Check the conflict is genuine.** Before asking the researcher to resolve it, check whether it depends on an equivocation (a term used in two senses), a scope difference, a suppressed premise, or a difference in time or context. If it does, say so; the conflict may dissolve on clarification.
3. **Make it the theme of the next round.** The theme is the inconsistent set. The questions open the routes:
   - which member the researcher would revise, and why that one;
   - what revising each member would cost the wider work;
   - whether the conflict rests on an equivocation or a scope difference;
   - whether the set misdescribes their view;
   - what their own examples suggest about which commitment is weakest.
4. **If the researcher disputes the conflict,** examine the equivocation or suppressed premise they point to. Their objection may be right.
5. **Once they choose a member to revise,** test the cost of that revision in the next round: does it break something elsewhere?
6. **Record the set and the repair** in the revision log.

### Example

```text
Your last answer adds a commitment that sits uneasily with an earlier one. You now hold:

1. Any classification scheme for the archive must be exhaustive: every document goes
   somewhere.
2. The categories must be mutually exclusive: no document goes in two places.
3. Some documents are both administrative and personal correspondence (you gave the
   1911 letters as an example).

These conflict only if the 1911 letters fall under both existing categories in the
same sense of "administrative", and if no third category is available.
```

The round that follows takes this set as its theme and asks the questions listed in step 3.

### Rules

- Never decide on the researcher's behalf which commitment goes.
- Never treat the failure of one thesis as support for its negation or for a particular rival.
- Never press a contradiction after a defensive exit (see `dialogue-safety.md`).
- An inconsistency the researcher cannot yet resolve is a legitimate result. Record it as open, with what would settle it.

## Part 2: classifying problems

Classify every weakness found in the session into one of these classes. Researchers act on them differently, and treating them alike produces either panic or complacency.

| Class | Meaning | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| **Fatal** | The conclusion cannot survive this problem in any version close to the current one | Abandon the claim, or replace it with a substantially different one |
| **Repairable** | A premise, definition, design element or step needs revision, after which the conclusion can stand | Make the specific repair and retest |
| **Scope-limiting** | The claim holds, but for fewer cases, settings or conditions than stated | Narrow the claim and say so explicitly |
| **Needs clarification** | Whether there is a problem depends on what the researcher means | Clarify the term or scope; the problem may vanish or become one of the other classes |
| **Needs evidence** | The claim may be true, but the support offered does not establish it | Find the evidence, run the test, cite the source, or weaken the claim to what is supported |
| **Needs argument** | A step is asserted where it needs to be argued | Supply the argument or the bridge premise |
| **Presentational** | The substance is sound; the writing obscures or overstates it | Rewrite |

Rules for classification:

- Classify the problem, not the claim. One claim may have a repairable problem and a scope-limiting one.
- Do not call a problem fatal unless every nearby repair has been considered. Most problems are repairable or scope-limiting.
- Do not count problems as evidence of weakness. One fatal problem outweighs any number of presentational ones; ten presentational problems do not add up to one fatal one.
- When the classification depends on the researcher's purpose (a problem that is fatal for a journal article may be scope-limiting for an exploratory essay), say so.

Each category skill names the problem patterns most common in its kind of research and their usual class.
